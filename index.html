Floor plan & experience design / 3,200 sq ft

One room, three speeds.

Traditional pool halls fail at mixed audiences because they are one undifferentiated room where the most serious players set the tone for everyone. The fix is not decor. It is zoning the floor so a couple on a first date, a crew of seven, and a league regular can all be in the same building at the same time without any of them feeling like they are in the wrong place.

The clearance math

Why five tables, not six

A 7-ft table has a 39 by 78 inch playing surface and an overall footprint of roughly 46 by 85 inches. Published room charts say a single 7-ft table wants a 13 by 16 foot room with a standard 58-inch cue, because a player needs about five feet of clear backstroke on every side.

Six times 13 by 16 is 1,248 square feet, and that is the number most first-time operators budget. It is also wrong. In a commercial bay, neighbouring tables share the five-foot band between them, so the real cost is footprint plus one shared aisle — about 190 sq ft per table in a 3-and-2 grid, or 940 sq ft for five.

The sixth table costs 160 square feet. The only place it can come from is the flex floor and eight booth seats.

Hold at five, and leave that slab clear, level and unobstructed with a power drop already in the floor. If blended utilization proves out above 35% for two consecutive quarters, table six installs in a weekend. If it does not, the space has been earning money as event floor the whole time.

Three physical constraints that are not negotiable. Ceiling at 10 ft clear minimum — published guidance puts 8 ft as the floor and 9 ft or more as ideal, and pendant fixtures hang 32 to 36 inches above the cloth. Slate tables sit on grade only, never an upper floor. And acoustics are the single most under-budgeted line in this category: baffles over the pool bay and the pod zone are the difference between a room that buzzes and a room nobody stays in.

Zoning logic

Three speeds, one room, no walls

Different groups want different intensity, and the mistake is forcing them all into the same intensity. Speed increases as you walk away from the door.

Speed 1 — Social

Front right, by the bar

Couples · first dates · solo arrivals · anyone waiting

Air hockey, high-tops, perch seating, the bar. Games here last four to ten minutes, not forty-five. No booking, no commitment, no equipment knowledge required. This is where someone who has never held a cue can stand with a drink and still feel like a participant rather than an audience.

Speed 2 — Group

Centre and left

Crews of 6–8 · birthdays · after-work groups

Four console pods and the booth wall. Pods have half-height banquette backs, not doors — a closed room kills the energy of the main floor and creates a supervision problem; a half-open pod gives a group its own territory while keeping it visible from the bar. Bookable in 60-minute blocks.

Speed 3 — Play

Rear bay

League players · regulars · serious practice

The three best-lit tables, furthest from traffic, with no walkway crossing a backstroke. League night lives here. Regulars get genuinely better playing conditions than anywhere else in the building — and they get them without setting the tone for the whole room, which is the entire point.

The one table everybody plays

Air hockey earns its 150 sq ft by sitting in the queue

A commercial 8-ft table measures 99.5 by 51.5 inches and weighs 435 lbs; the 7-ft version is 84 by 44 inches at 380 lbs. With three feet clear behind each goal and three at the sides, the 7-ft table needs a 10 by 15 foot alcove. That is five percent of the floor for a two-player game, which only makes sense if it is placed correctly.

Two placement rules, both counter-intuitive:

  • Never adjacent to the pool bay. A blower running at volume, pucks hitting rails, and a crowd that shouts and lunges is the worst possible neighbour for someone lining up a shot. Worse, spectators spill directly into cue clearance. Put a solid wall or the full width of the lounge between them.
  • Put it on the waiting path. A three-minute game converts wait time into dwell time, and dwell time into a second drink. On a Friday with every table booked, the group standing at the host desk is either playing air hockey and ordering, or they are leaving. This is the highest-return 150 square feet in the building and it has almost nothing to do with air hockey.
Price it as a hook, not a line item. Free with any table or pod booking; a $2 token otherwise. The revenue is not in the table — it is in the fact that nobody who plays air hockey is standing around deciding whether to stay. Budget a refurbished commercial unit at roughly $4,000 CAD rather than a new coin-operated model, which lists between US$5,695 and US$9,290 before freight and duty.

The first fifteen feet

A visitor who cannot answer three questions will leave

What does it cost, where do I sit, and where is the washroom. If a first-time visitor can answer all three without speaking to anyone, they stay. If they cannot, they stand in the doorway for eleven seconds and go somewhere else. That is the whole brief for the entry sequence.

  • Sightline order from the door: host stand, price board, bar, air hockey. In that sequence. What a visitor must not see first is a wall of strangers' backs bent over tables — which is precisely what every traditional hall opens with, and precisely why its audience self-selected down to people who already play.
  • Washrooms within 40 feet of the entry, signposted from the door, two single-occupancy barrier-free rooms rather than one multi-stall. Nobody should have to cross the entire floor, past every table, to find them.
  • The pool bay is the furthest thing from the door by design. It is the most intimidating element for a newcomer and the most sensitive to interruption for a regular. Distance solves both problems with one decision.
  • One staff position at the bar sees 90% of the floor. This reads as a comfort and safety feature, and it is — but it is also how a 3,200 sq ft room runs on two people on a Tuesday, which is what holds labour at roughly 30% of revenue.

Inclusion mechanics

Cheap fixes that almost nobody does

None of this is decor and none of it is expensive. It is the gap between a room that technically welcomes everybody and a room where everybody actually stays.

  • A cue wall with 52-inch and 58-inch cues in 18 to 21 oz weights. A 58-inch cue is the published standard, but it is genuinely unplayable for shorter players, and equipment that does not fit is the fastest way to tell somebody a room is not for them. Cost: under $900
  • Sound capped at 78–80 dB in the social zone. Couples leave rooms where they cannot hear each other, and the loss is invisible because they never complain — they just do not return. A locked limiter on the mixer and a meter behind the bar. Cost: under $400
  • Lighting in three deliberate layers. Warm, honest, bright task light over the cloth; dim perimeter; one accent feature wall. Bright, well-stocked washrooms with proper mirrors and locks. Not a black box.
  • No seat with an open back to a walkway. Booth backs, wall seating, or half-height screens throughout. Nobody relaxes in a room where they can be approached from behind without seeing it.
  • A table-side QR to a 60-second rules video and a one-line house-rules card. Staff offer a two-minute intro once, then leave. Beginners want permission to be bad at something, not supervision.
  • A code of conduct at the door and in every booking confirmation, and any staff member empowered to move or comp a group without finding a manager. The policy matters less than the speed of the response.
  • A non-alcoholic list designed and priced like the cocktail list. In the Scarborough–Ajax catchment this is not a concession, it is a primary revenue line — and it is the reason the venue can open profitably before the liquor licence lands.

Programming

Every night needs a reason to exist

Peak Friday and Saturday evenings will run 80–90% occupancy on their own. Tuesday at 3pm will run near zero. The business is won in the gap, and the gap is filled by programming, not by marketing spend.

Wednesday is the most important night of the week and it looks like the least important. An alternate-shot mixed-pairs format is the single most effective inclusion mechanic in billiards: it structurally requires mixed teams, it hides skill gaps because partners alternate shots, and it turns a solo regular into a pair who both now have a standing Wednesday commitment. It builds the cohort that fills Tuesday and Thursday.

Pricing architecture

Price the group, not the person

Per-person pricing punishes exactly the customers this venue is trying to win — a couple, or a group where two people play and four watch. Charge for the asset and let the group self-organise around it.

Capex delta

What this layout costs against the $174,200 base

Adding air hockey and doing the acoustics properly costs about $9,300. Funding it by cutting contingency is the obvious move and the wrong one.

Flag this in the case study rather than hiding it. A $6,000 contingency on a $150,000 build is about 4%, and the honest range is 10–15%. The defensible version finances the air hockey table and the acoustic package on a 36-month equipment lease at roughly $280 a month, keeps contingency at $13,000, and accepts slightly thinner monthly cash flow in exchange for surviving the first unbudgeted surprise — which, in a second-generation space, always arrives.
